# US Law Source Hierarchy

## Purpose

Use this hierarchy to distinguish binding authority, official publication, current editorial compilations, persuasive authority, and informational material.

## Core Rule

For legal conclusions, prefer primary sources and record the date, jurisdiction, court, agency, and publication status.

## Federal Sources

### Statutes

Use:

1. enacted law or Statutes at Large when exact enacted text matters;
2. United States Code for codified general and permanent federal laws;
3. Congress.gov for legislative history, bill status, and public-law tracking;
4. GovInfo for authenticated federal publications and downloadable packages.

Important distinction:

- the United States Code is a subject-matter codification prepared by the Office of the Law Revision Counsel;
- the Statutes at Large preserve enacted public and private laws chronologically.

### Regulations

Use:

1. official Federal Register PDF for the published rule, notice, or proposed rule;
2. annual official Code of Federal Regulations publication on GovInfo when official edition matters;
3. eCFR for continuously updated editorial access and point-in-time research;
4. issuing agency website for guidance, forms, manuals, FAQs, policy memoranda, and procedural updates.

Important distinction:

- CFR is the official codification;
- eCFR is continuously updated but is not an official legal edition.

### Executive And Presidential Material

Use:

- Federal Register;
- White House official publications when applicable;
- GovInfo collections;
- agency implementation material.

### Federal Court Material

Use:

- Supreme Court website for slip opinions and U.S. Reports;
- issuing federal court website for local rules and orders;
- PACER for federal dockets and filed documents;
- CourtListener as a useful search and alert layer, not a substitute for official verification.

### Federal Rules Of Procedure

Use:

- U.S. Courts official rules pages;
- Supreme Court rules when applicable;
- local rules of the relevant court;
- standing orders and judge-specific procedures when relevant.

## State And Local Sources

Before relying on a rule, identify:

- state or territory;
- county;
- city or municipality;
- court system;
- agency;
- effective date;
- whether local rules or ordinances also apply.

Use official state legislature, state administrative code, state court, attorney general, agency, county, and municipal websites whenever available.

Use USAGov state-government pages as a directory to official state and territory sites.

## Case-Law Treatment

For every important case, record:

- court;
- date;
- citation;
- procedural posture;
- holding;
- relevant reasoning;
- whether the case is binding or persuasive;
- subsequent history;
- negative treatment or overruling risk;
- jurisdiction match.

Do not rely on a case citation until treatment is checked through an appropriate citator or later-case review.

## Secondary Sources

Secondary sources may accelerate research but do not replace primary-source verification.

Examples:

- treatises;
- practice guides;
- law-review articles;
- agency explainers;
- legal-aid resources;
- bar-association guidance;
- commercial legal databases;
- reputable legal analysis.

## Search Snippets And AI Output

Never treat search snippets, summaries, or AI output as authority.

Use them only to locate sources and formulate research questions.

## Final Rule

Cite the controlling source, preserve the publication context, and state when a source is editorial, persuasive, unofficial, or not yet verified.
